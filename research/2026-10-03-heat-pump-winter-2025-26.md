# Heat pump, winter 2025–26: what it cost, where efficiency went, and a model for the optimiser

*Window Watch research note, 3 October 2026. Derived from Vaillant hourly energy buckets for
1 Oct 2025 – 25 Apr 2026 (4,968 hours, none missing), backfilled with
`energy.py vaillant --start --end`. Weather is the Open-Meteo hourly archive for the flat's
coordinates. Prices are Octopus Cosy (`COSY-22-12-08`, region C, assumed — confirm against a
bill), half-hourly as published. Vaillant's energy figures are its own model estimates, not
metered; treat them as ±10–20%.*

---

## TL;DR

- **Heat pump electricity last winter: 1,116 kWh, £273** (heating £233, hot water £41), at an
  average **24.5p/kWh**. Only 41% of kWh landed in cheap slots; 12% fell at peak and cost
  £58 on its own.
- **With the battery buying everything at the cheap rate**, at the measured 93% round trip, the
  same usage would have cost **£169, saving ~£104 a winter.**
- **Seasonal heating COP Nov–Mar: 3.48.** Outdoor temperature drives it (2.7 below 0°C, 4.7
  above 12°C). Output level is a lever: **62% of heat was delivered in hours above 2 kW,
  where COP runs ~0.3–0.5 lower than at 1–2 kW at the same temperature.** That's the cost of
  the boost/recovery schedule, worth roughly 9% of heating electricity.
- Defrost conditions (−1 to 6°C, RH ≥ 85%) show no extra penalty. Standby (~7 W) is only 1.8%
  of heating electricity.
- **The 2024–25 vs 2025–26 comparison is still open.** The previous winter's hourly backfill hit
  Vaillant's multi-day quota and hasn't run yet.

**Battery scenario.** The SolaX battery (in service from May 2026) charges at the cheap rate and
covers the gaps. Measured since May: 766 kWh in, 712 kWh out, a 93% round trip. Buying all
1,116 kWh at the cheap rate and dividing by 0.93 gives **£169**. That ignores the rest of the
flat's load competing for the 10.5 kWh. Section 4 checks it fits.

**By output, at matched temperature.** COP peaks at 1–2 kW and falls above it:

| °C \ Wh/h | 500–1000 | 1000–1500 | 1500–2000 | > 2000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3–6 | 3.49 | 3.57 | 3.68 | 3.35 |

**Hour of day (GMT).** The worst COP is at 06:00 (3.05, coldest air), and the 08:00–09:00
recovery after the 10°C overnight setback runs at 3.2–3.4. The boosts at 13:00 and 22:00 put
1.5 kWh/h into the system at COP 3.5. The schedule pushed heat into exactly the
high-output, cold-air corner of the map.

**Monthly COP**: Nov 3.35, Dec 3.76, Jan 3.38, Feb 3.34, Mar 3.65. Nov and Feb ran low for
their mean temperatures (9.4°C, 8.0°C), and Dec high for its 7.8°C. Not explained here; the
2024–25 comparison may help.

## 3. Model for the optimiser

Weighted by heat, fitted on heating hours:

```
1/COP = 0.3067 − 0.0095·T_out(°C) + 0.0128·P_heat(kW)
```

| | 1 kW | 2 kW | 3 kW |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0°C | 3.13 | 3.00 | 2.89 |
| 5°C | 3.68 | 3.51 | 3.36 |
| 10°C | 4.47 | 4.22 | 4.00 |

Practical reading: **run long and gentle, and lean towards the warmer part of the day.** Moving
a kWh of heat from 2 kW at dawn (~3°C) to 1 kW in the afternoon (~8°C) cuts its electricity
by ~20% (COP 3.29 → 4.11). With the battery, the timing no longer has to chase price, so efficiency and comfort
can decide when the heat pump runs.

## 4. Does the battery cover it?

The coldest month (Jan) averaged 8.5 kWh/day of heat pump electricity, ~18 kWh on the coldest
days. The longest gaps between cheap slots are 6 h (07–13 and 16–22). At ~0.75 kW average plus
~0.3 kW house load, a 6 h gap is ~6.3 kWh against 10.5 kWh usable. It fits, but on the coldest
days the 16–22 gap will be close if the heat pump runs hard then. That's another reason to
keep output steady rather than boosting.

Battery behaviour observed on 3 Oct 2026 (solax.csv): it discharged all night with grid at 0,
then at 04:00 charged at ~3 kW from 88% to 99% in under an hour, topped up slowly, and the flat
ran from the grid until 07:00 (cheap). From 07:00 it was back on battery with grid at 0.
**Sign conventions confirmed: `gridPower` negative = importing; `chargeDischargePower`
positive = charging.**
